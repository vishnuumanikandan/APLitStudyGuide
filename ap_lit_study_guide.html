How the Exam Works

Section I — Multiple Choice

55 questions · 60 minutes · 45% of score

5 passages (poetry & prose) · close-reading

Section II — Free Response

3 essays · 120 minutes · 55% of score

Q1 Poetry · Q2 Prose · Q3 Open

The Rubric (per essay)

6 points total

  • Thesis (0–1)
  • Evidence + Commentary (0–4)
  • Sophistication (0–1)

The 6-point trap. The single hardest point to earn is Sophistication. It is given for complexity of thought, not flowery vocabulary. Engage tensions, contradictions, ambiguities — see The Complex Thesis and the Open Prompt Playbook.

The Complex Thesis

Since 2019, every AP Lit FRQ uses the word “complex.” A complex idea, character, relationship, response, experience, or perspective is multifaceted, nuanced, layered, contradictory — never one-dimensional.

Thesis Formula

\[ \text{Thesis}_\text{complex} \;=\; \underbrace{\text{Defensible Claim}}_{\text{interpretation}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{Tension}}_{\text{paradox / contradiction}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{So-What}}_{\text{meaning of work as a whole}} \]

Complex attitude / perspective Ambivalence: admiration tempered by skepticism, pride blended with guilt.

Complex response A blend of emotional and intellectual reactions held simultaneously.

Complex experience Coexistence of joy & sorrow, triumph & failure, growth & pain.

Complex relationship Contrasting emotions (love + resentment); shifting dynamics (dependence → independence).

Complex characterization Conflicting traits (bravery + insecurity), or evolution (naïveté → cynicism).

Example — Built from The Great Gatsby

Although Nick Carraway frames Gatsby as a man of “extraordinary gift for hope,” his narration is a slow act of self-betrayal: by canonizing Gatsby, Nick disguises his own complicity in the very Eastern decadence he claims to flee, so that the novel’s “meaning as a whole” becomes a confession masquerading as eulogy.

Why it works — claim ✓ · tension (admiration vs. complicity) ✓ · so-what (genre-bending: eulogy → confession) ✓.

Literary Devices: The Must-Know Toolkit

Curated from the Poetic Terms Toolkit and the Poetry Foundation Glossary of Poetic Terms. Devices are grouped by function — exactly how AP graders expect you to talk about them.

Sound & Music

Alliteration — repetition of initial consonant sounds. Effect: emphasis, mood, mnemonic binding.

Assonance — repetition of internal vowel sounds. Effect: melody; slows the line.

Consonance — repetition of consonant sounds (often terminal). Effect: roughness, slant rhyme.

Cacophony — deliberately harsh, awkward sounds. Effect: dissonance, violence, unease.

Euphony — pleasing, smooth sound. Effect: harmony, beauty, calm.

Onomatopoeia — words that imitate sound. Effect: sensory immediacy.

Repetition & Pattern

Anaphora — repetition at the beginning of successive clauses. Effect: incantation, escalation.

Epistrophe — repetition at the end of successive clauses. Effect: emphasis on a fixed idea.

Refrain — line that recurs at intervals. Effect: returning theme; ritual.

Polysyndeton — extra conjunctions (“and… and… and…”). Effect: weight, biblical pace, accumulation.

Asyndeton — conjunctions omitted. Effect: speed, urgency, breathlessness.

Chiasmus — crossed parallel: \(a:b \to b:a\). Effect: balance, reversal, revelation.

Figure & Trope

Metaphor — implicit comparison without “like / as.”

Simile — explicit comparison with “like” or “as.”

Conceit — extended, often surprising or paradoxical metaphor.

Synecdoche — part for whole (“all hands on deck”).

Metonymy — associated thing for the thing (“the Crown” = monarchy).

Personification — abstract / non-human given human traits.

Apostrophe — direct address to the absent / dead / inanimate.

Allusion — reference to another text, event, or figure.

Symbol — image standing for a larger abstract idea.

Allegory — sustained dual-level narrative (literal + symbolic).

Synaesthesia — mixing of senses (“loud color,” “warm sound”).

Hyperbole / Litotes — exaggeration / understatement.

Structure, Lineation, Pace

Caesura — audible pause inside a line, often punctuated.

End-stopped line — line ending with terminal punctuation; meaning is closed.

Enjambment — sentence runs over a line break; meaning carries forward.

Stanza — grouped lines with white space between (couplet, tercet, quatrain…).

Volta — the turn in a sonnet (after line 8 in Petrarchan; line 12 in Shakespearean).

Climax — peak of tension or intensity.

Voice, Tone, Irony

Speaker — the “I” of the poem; the persona, not the author.

Diction — word choice (formal, colloquial, archaic, monosyllabic, Latinate…).

Tone — speaker’s attitude (reverent, ironic, elegiac, sardonic…).

Mood — reader’s emotional response (eerie, hopeful, oppressive…).

Verbal irony — saying the opposite of what is meant.

Situational irony — outcome contradicts expectation.

Dramatic irony — audience knows what character does not.

Speech act — the manner of expression: plea, command, vow, confession.

Forms (poetry)

Sonnet (14 lines, iambic pentameter) — Petrarchan (8+6) or Shakespearean (4+4+4+2).

Villanelle — 19 lines, two refrains, \(aba\) tercets + closing quatrain.

Elegy — reflective lament for a loss.

Ode — long, elevated lyric meditation.

Dramatic monologue — speaker addresses a silent listener; reveals self unawares.

Free verse — no fixed meter or rhyme; rhythm of speech.

AP rule of thumb. Don’t catalog devices — interpret them. Every device citation should be followed by “…which produces / suggests / reveals…” The grader rewards function, not vocabulary.

Scansion & Prosody

Use KaTeX-style notation. Mark stressed as \(\acute{x}\) (or \(/\)) and unstressed as \(\breve{x}\) (or \(\smile\)).

The Common Feet

\[ \begin{array}{lll} \text{Iamb} & \breve{\;}\;\acute{\;} & \text{a-}\textbf{way} \\[2pt] \text{Trochee} & \acute{\;}\;\breve{\;} & \textbf{ti}\text{-ger} \\[2pt] \text{Anapest} & \breve{\;}\;\breve{\;}\;\acute{\;} & \text{in-the-}\textbf{night} \\[2pt] \text{Dactyl} & \acute{\;}\;\breve{\;}\;\breve{\;} & \textbf{mer}\text{-ri-ly} \\[2pt] \text{Spondee} & \acute{\;}\;\acute{\;} & \textbf{stop! stop!} \\[2pt] \text{Pyrrhic} & \breve{\;}\;\breve{\;} & \text{of the} \end{array} \]

Line Lengths

\[ \text{Line length} = n \cdot \text{foot}, \quad n \in \{1\!:\!\text{mono},\; 2\!:\!\text{di},\; 3\!:\!\text{tri},\; 4\!:\!\text{tetra},\; 5\!:\!\text{penta},\; 6\!:\!\text{hexa}\} \]

So iambic pentameter = \(5 \times (\breve{\;}\;\acute{\;})\) — Shakespeare’s heartbeat, ten syllables per line, alternating unstressed/stressed.

Worked Example

Shakespeare, Sonnet 18, line 1:

\[ \underbrace{\breve{\text{Shall}}\;\acute{\text{I}}}_{\text{iamb}}\;\Big|\; \underbrace{\breve{\text{com-}}\;\acute{\text{pare}}}_{\text{iamb}}\;\Big|\; \underbrace{\breve{\text{thee}}\;\acute{\text{to}}}_{\text{iamb}}\;\Big|\; \underbrace{\breve{\text{a}}\;\acute{\text{sum-}}}_{\text{iamb}}\;\Big|\; \underbrace{\breve{\text{mer's}}\;\acute{\text{day}}}_{\text{iamb}} \]

Five iambic feet → iambic pentameter. Because the lines are unrhymed in many Shakespeare passages, it is also blank verse.

On the FRQ. You are never required to scan in your essay — but identifying meter (and where it breaks) earns sophistication. Search for substitutions: a sudden trochee or spondee in iambic pentameter is the poem yelling at you.

The Seven Works

The Great Gatsby

F. Scott Fitzgerald, 1925

Genre — Modernist novel, social tragedy Setting — Long Island & NYC, Summer 1922 POV — First-person, Nick Carraway (unreliable, retrospective) Form — 9 chapters, frame narration Movement — Jazz Age modernism

Why it dominates AP open prompts: symbolic house, idealism, reinvented identity, the past, the American Dream, morally ambiguous protagonist, illuminating party scenes — Gatsby is a Swiss-army-knife answer.

Plot in 90 Seconds

Nick Carraway, a Yale-educated bond salesman, moves to West Egg next door to the mysterious millionaire Jay Gatsby (born James Gatz). Across the bay in East Egg live Nick’s cousin Daisy and her brutish, philandering husband Tom Buchanan. Gatsby has built his fortune (largely through bootlegging) to recapture Daisy, his lost love from before WWI. Tom’s mistress Myrtle is killed by Daisy driving Gatsby’s car; her husband George, manipulated by Tom, murders Gatsby and then himself. Nick, disgusted with the East, returns to the Midwest.

Characters

Character Function Key Trait
Jay Gatsby (James Gatz) Tragic idealist; reinvented self “Extraordinary gift for hope”
Nick Carraway Unreliable narrator, moral compass with smudged edges “Within and without”
Daisy Buchanan The “golden girl”; voice “full of money” Carelessness as privilege
Tom Buchanan Antagonist; old-money brutality Racism, infidelity, force
Jordan Baker Foil to Daisy; modern New Woman “Incurably dishonest”
Myrtle Wilson Tragic mistress; class-climber Vitality crushed by carelessness
George Wilson Foil to Gatsby; working-class believer Dust-grey despair
Meyer Wolfsheim Source of Gatsby’s wealth Anti-Semitic stereotype to interrogate

Themes & Motifs

  • The American Dream as both aspiration and indictment.
  • Time & the past: Gatsby’s “Can’t repeat the past? Why of course you can!”
  • Class: Old money vs. new money vs. no money (the Valley of Ashes).
  • Carelessness: “They were careless people, Tom and Daisy.”
  • Performance & spectacle: the parties as theater.

Symbols

Green light — futurity, desire, the orgastic future that recedes.

Eyes of T. J. Eckleburg — eroded god watching the moral wasteland.

Valley of Ashes — industrial & moral decay between Eggs and city.

Gatsby’s mansion — the house (perfect for 2021 prompt) as façade.

Yellow / gold — corrupted wealth (“yellow cocktail music,” yellow car).

White — Daisy & Jordan’s faux-purity, illusion of innocence.

Form & Style

Frame narrative — Nick writes in 1924 about the summer of 1922; everything is filtered through retrospect. Fitzgerald’s prose moves between lyric romanticism (the green light, the orgastic future) and wry social satire (Tom’s racism, the party guest list of Chapter 4). Watch for synesthesia (“yellow cocktail music”), anaphora in Gatsby’s idealist speeches, and the famous closing chiasmus-adjacent rhythm: “borne back ceaselessly into the past.”

Killer Quotes

  • “So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past.”
  • “He smiled understandingly — much more than understandingly.”
  • “Her voice is full of money.”
  • “I was within and without, simultaneously enchanted and repelled.”

Open-Prompt Playbook

Prompt Family Angle
House as symbol (2021) Gatsby’s mansion: a stage set for an audience of one.
Reinvention (2023-1) James Gatz → Jay Gatsby: identity as architecture.
Idealism’s cost (2019) Gatsby’s “ideal view” both ennobles and destroys him.
Past (2025-1, 2007) Gatsby’s pathology: the past as a horizon, not a memory.
Morally ambiguous (2002) Gatsby’s bootlegging vs. his “incorruptible dream.”

Sample Complex Thesis

Fitzgerald’s Gatsby is structured as a eulogy that secretly indicts its own narrator: by elevating Gatsby’s “extraordinary gift for hope” into a private religion, Nick Carraway transforms Daisy’s carelessness into Gatsby’s tragedy and his own complicity into nostalgic prose, so that the green light marks not a lost lover but the unsayable truth that the American Dream survives only as a story we agree to tell about the dead.

Home Fire

Kamila Shamsie, 2017

Genre — Contemporary novel; political reimagining of Sophocles’ Antigone Setting — London, Massachusetts, Istanbul, Raqqa, Karachi (post-2014) POV — Five rotating third-person sections (one per main character) Form — 5 parts: Isma · Eamonn · Parvaiz · Aneeka · Karamat Awards — Women’s Prize for Fiction, 2018

Why it dominates AP open prompts: justice, citizenship, secrets, family vs. state, cultural collision, the tragic-hero structure of Antigone in 21st-century clothes. The Complex Thesis PDF references Aneeka, Eamonn, Parvaiz, and Karamat directly.

Plot in 90 Seconds

Isma Pasha leaves her younger siblings — twins Aneeka and Parvaiz — to study in the U.S. In Massachusetts she meets Eamonn, son of Karamat Lone, the British Home Secretary (who has aggressively distanced himself from the Muslim community). Eamonn returns to London and falls for Aneeka, who has been searching for a way to recover her brother. Parvaiz, groomed by ISIS recruiters who exploited stories about his jihadi father, has gone to Raqqa as a media operative; horrified, he flees to Istanbul to come home — and is killed at the British consulate. Karamat, as Home Secretary, strips Parvaiz’s citizenship even in death and refuses repatriation of the body. Aneeka flies to Pakistan to mourn her brother in the open desert; Eamonn follows; the novel ends in their joint immolation, fulfilling the Antigone arc.

Characters

Character Antigone parallel Function
Isma Pasha Ismene The cautious survivor; sister-as-mother
Aneeka Pasha Antigone Defies state for ritual love of brother
Parvaiz Pasha Polynices The “traitor” denied burial
Eamonn Lone Haemon Lover caught between father and beloved
Karamat Lone Creon The state; the assimilated Muslim ruler
Terry Lone Eurydice Karamat’s American wife

Themes & Motifs

  • Citizenship & belonging: Who counts as British? The Pasha siblings’ father was a jihadi; Karamat says: “You are, we are, British.”
  • Family vs. state — the Antigone axis.
  • Surveillance — Aneeka knows the airport, the visa, the watch.
  • Performance of identity — Karamat’s un-Muslim Muslimness; Eamonn’s hyphenated upbringing.
  • Grief as politics — denial of burial = denial of personhood.
  • Twin-ship — Aneeka & Parvaiz: bodies as one.

Symbols

Hijab — chosen identity vs. assigned identity; Aneeka removes/wears strategically.

The corpse — Parvaiz’s unburied body = the Antigone center.

Heathrow security room — the threshold of citizenship.

The Karachi park / Pakistani sun — exposure, ritual, refusal of secrecy.

Father’s empty chair — the inherited absence.

Karamat’s accent — performed assimilation; the price of power.

Form & Style

The five-part structure forces shifting empathy: each section briefly aligns the reader with the character whose name titles it, including Karamat — making villainy structurally complicated. Shamsie weaves in embedded media: tweets, news headlines, Skype messages, and a final televised scene. The closing tableau is written in the present tense — time collapses into ritual.

Killer Quotes

  • “The ones we love are enemies of the state.” (Aneeka, quoting / refusing Sophocles)
  • “You are, we are, British.” (Karamat)
  • “Justice is to give to each what they are due.”

Open-Prompt Playbook

Prompt Family Angle
Justice (2011) Aneeka’s private justice vs. Karamat’s public justice.
Cultural collision (2003-B) British Muslim identity under post-9/11 surveillance.
Hierarchy (2022) The Home Secretary literalizes state hierarchy.
Rebel (2023-2) Aneeka publicly mourns to weaponize her grief.
Secrets (2025-2) Parvaiz’s recruitment; Eamonn hides Aneeka from his father.
Confidant(e) (1992) Isma as Ismene-confidante; Eamonn confides what Aneeka cannot say.
Tragic vision (2003) Parvaiz as instrument of universal suffering.

Sample Complex Thesis

Shamsie’s Home Fire re-stages Sophocles to argue that the modern state’s deepest violence is taxonomic: by making citizenship legible only as performed assimilation, Karamat Lone’s policy converts the private grief of the Pasha twins into a public ritual that the state cannot answer except with denial — so that the novel’s incandescent ending insists love itself becomes a political act when the law refuses to recognize it as such.

The Catcher in the Rye

J. D. Salinger, 1951

Genre — Bildungsroman, post-war American novel Setting — New York City, three days, December 1949 POV — First-person retrospective, Holden Caulfield (institutionalized as he tells it) Form — 26 chapters, conversational

Why it dominates AP open prompts: the textbook bildungsroman; outward conformity / inward questioning; gift-as-burden; alienation. Holden is the patron saint of any prompt about adolescent disquiet.

Plot in 90 Seconds

Sixteen-year-old Holden Caulfield, expelled from Pencey Prep, drifts through Manhattan over three days — checking into a hotel, meeting old friends and a prostitute, getting beaten up by a pimp, drinking too much, calling a former teacher, sneaking home to see his sister Phoebe, and finally watching her ride a carousel in the rain. He narrates from a sanatorium in California. The trauma underwriting everything is the death of his younger brother Allie from leukemia.

Characters

Character Function
Holden Caulfield Unreliable, traumatized, deeply moral narrator
Phoebe Caulfield Moral conscience; the “catcher” Holden cannot be
Allie Caulfield Dead brother; the original wound
D. B. Caulfield Older brother who “sold out” to Hollywood
Mr. Antolini Ambiguous mentor; speech on falling vs. dying
Stradlater / Ackley Foils — the phony jock; the gross intimate
Sally Hayes / Jane Gallagher The girl Holden performs vs. the girl he loves

Themes & Motifs

  • Phoniness — Holden’s diagnostic for adult performance.
  • Innocence & its loss — the museum, the children in the field, Phoebe.
  • Death — Allie, James Castle, the funeral imagery, the “catcher” fantasy.
  • Alienation as self-protection.
  • Language as armor — Holden’s verbal tics (“if you really want to know,” “and all”).

Symbols

Red hunting hat — armor; visibility on his terms.

Allie’s baseball mitt — grief inscribed in green ink.

The catcher in the rye — fantasy of arresting time.

Museum of Natural History — frozen, unchanging childhood.

Carousel & the gold ring — Phoebe’s grasp at growing up.

The ducks in the lagoon — what survives the freeze?

Form & Style

The novel’s voice is its formal innovation: first-person colloquial with diction that catalogs class, region, and trauma at once. Watch for anaphora of “and,” epistrophe of “and all,” repetition of “phony,” and the relentless direct address (“if you want to know the truth”) that turns the reader into a confessor. The retrospective frame (chapter 1 and the last paragraph) reframes everything as a clinical narrative.

Killer Quotes

  • “If a body catch a body coming through the rye.”
  • “Don’t ever tell anybody anything. If you do, you start missing everybody.”
  • “The mark of the immature man is that he wants to die nobly for a cause, while the mark of the mature man is that he wants to live humbly for one.”

Open-Prompt Playbook

Prompt Family Angle
Bildungsroman (2013) The carousel scene = the pivotal moment Holden lets Phoebe go.
Outward conform / inward question (2005) Holden in adult NYC: jacket, tie, ulcer of feeling.
Madness w/ a discerning eye (2001) The narrator-from-the-sanatorium frame.
Indecision (2024-1) Three days of not deciding — the novel is the procrastination.
Unfulfilled need (2024-2) Holden cannot name the loss of Allie.
Gift / burden (2018) Holden’s hyper-perception is gift and curse.

Sample Complex Thesis

Salinger weaponizes vernacular: Holden’s compulsive phony is the only word allowed in a grammar of grief that refuses both Allie’s name and the verb died, so that the novel’s apparent rejection of adulthood is a more difficult acceptance — that growing up is what one does with loss, not over it, and the carousel’s gold ring is the small risk Holden finally permits Phoebe to take in his place.

Death of a Salesman

Arthur Miller, 1949

Genre — Modern tragedy; “play in two acts and a Requiem” Setting — Brooklyn (Loman home) & Boston, late 1940s; expressionist past intrudes on present Form — 2 acts + Requiem Pulitzer Prize for Drama — 1949

Why it dominates AP open prompts: Miller’s “tragedy of the common man,” symbolic house hemmed in by apartments, illusion vs. reality, fathers and sons, idealism’s cost, the “American” prompt par excellence.

Plot in 90 Seconds

Willy Loman, a 63-year-old traveling salesman, returns home exhausted. His sons Biff and Happy are in town; Biff, once a high-school football star, has wandered for years since discovering Willy’s affair in a Boston hotel. Over 24 hours, Willy is fired, borrows money from his neighbor Charley to pretend he’s still earning, hallucinates conversations with his successful brother Ben, and finally crashes his car so Biff can collect $20,000 in life insurance. The Requiem at the grave: Linda doesn’t understand why no one came; Charley delivers the eulogy. Biff says, “He had the wrong dreams. All, all wrong.”

Characters

Character Function
Willy Loman Tragic everyman; mind oscillates between past and present
Linda Loman Devoted, clear-eyed; “Attention must be paid.”
Biff Loman Lost son; failed promise; only one who finally sees
Happy Loman Womanizing, hollow; doomed to repeat Willy
Ben Loman Mythic brother — diamonds, jungle, success as conquest
Charley Neighbor; pragmatic foil to Willy’s dreams
Bernard Charley’s son; quiet success vs. Biff’s promise
The Woman (Boston) Willy’s affair; the moment that breaks Biff

Themes & Motifs

  • The American Dream as delusion — being “well-liked” ≠ being valuable.
  • Fathers and sons — Willy → Biff is a chain of broken inheritances.
  • Memory invading the present — the past is staged, not narrated.
  • Pride and shame — Willy will borrow but won’t accept Charley’s job.
  • Nature vs. industry — flute, seeds, “boxed in” by apartment buildings.

Symbols

Seeds — Willy’s late-night planting: nothing has grown.

The flute — Willy’s father; pastoral lost America.

Diamonds (Ben’s) — wealth as wilderness conquest.

The stockings — Linda’s mended pair vs. The Woman’s gift.

The house & yard — the home boxed in (perfect 2021 prompt). :—————— |

Football & sneakers — Biff’s lost golden hour.

Form & Style

Miller’s stage directions create an expressionist set: transparent walls, rooms that the past walks through. Time is non-linear — Willy’s memories bleed into the action without warning, dramatized for the audience as if they were happening now. The play’s title is a critical move: Death of a Salesman names the genre (a vocational obituary) before the man. Watch for anaphora in Willy’s catalog of customers, and the famous epistrophe “well-liked” / “well-liked.”

Killer Quotes

  • “Attention, attention must finally be paid to such a person.”
  • “I am not a dime a dozen! I am Willy Loman, and you are Biff Loman!”
  • “He had the wrong dreams. All, all wrong.”
  • “A salesman is got to dream, boy. It comes with the territory.”

Open-Prompt Playbook

Prompt Family Angle
Symbolic house (2021) The Loman home, “boxed in.”
Tragic vision (2003) Willy as tragic hero whose suffering radiates.
Time manipulation (1986) Memory staged as present action.
Misconception of self (1968 / 1982-2) Willy’s illusion perpetuated into death.
Father-son conflict (1990) Boston hotel = the wound.
Indecision (2024-1) Biff’s seventeen-year drift; Willy’s fatal late-night yard scene.
Past affecting present (2007) Memory is the play’s machinery.

Sample Complex Thesis

Miller’s expressionist staging — walls that memory walks through — proves that Willy Loman’s tragedy is not simply that he believed the wrong dream but that the dream’s grammar required belief without evidence: each remembered scene rehearses Willy’s fatal substitution of being liked for being known, so that his suicide is less a defeat than the literalization of a vocation already defined as the disappearance of self into transaction.

The Scarlet Letter

Nathaniel Hawthorne, 1850

Genre — Romance (in Hawthorne’s sense), psychological allegory Setting — Puritan Boston, 1640s; framed by 1850 “Custom-House” essay POV — Third-person omniscient; ironic narrator Form — Custom-House preface + 24 chapters

Why it dominates AP open prompts: symbol (the letter A itself), public hierarchy, secrecy, sin & justice, alienated heroine, allegory, Puritan-as-cultural-collision. Strong for almost any “morally ambiguous” or “outward conform / inward question” prompt.

Plot in 90 Seconds

Hester Prynne, a married Englishwoman whose husband had not arrived in the New World, gives birth to a daughter (Pearl). Refusing to name her lover, she is sentenced to wear a scarlet “A” on her chest. Her aged husband arrives under the alias Roger Chillingworth and devotes himself to discovering and destroying her lover — the saintly young minister Arthur Dimmesdale. Over seven years Hester rebuilds a life of quiet service; Dimmesdale rots from within; Chillingworth becomes a fiend. In the climactic scaffold scene Dimmesdale confesses publicly and dies; Chillingworth dies of self-consumption; Hester departs but eventually returns to live alone with the letter as her own.

Characters

Character Function
Hester Prynne Sinner-saint; the letter as identity reclaimed
Arthur Dimmesdale Hidden sinner; embodiment of secret guilt
Roger Chillingworth The leech; vengeance personified
Pearl “Living scarlet letter”; nature & truth
Mistress Hibbins Witch; foil revealing Puritan repressed shadow
Governor Bellingham The state; civic Puritan authority

Themes & Motifs

  • Sin: public vs. private — Hester’s punishment is visible; Dimmesdale’s interior.
  • Hypocrisy of the Puritan social order.
  • Identity through stigma — the letter shifts meaning: Adulteress → Able → Angel.
  • Nature vs. law — the forest vs. the town; the brook; the meteor.
  • Allegory — Hawthorne’s narrator practically winks at his own symbols.

Symbols

The scarlet “A” — sign whose meaning the wearer rewrites.

Pearl — animate symbol; “the scarlet letter, endowed with life.”

The scaffold — public stage of judgment (three scenes structure the novel).

The forest — moral lawlessness and freedom.

The meteor (A in the sky) — symbol authored by the guilty mind.

Rosebush at the prison door — pity inside punishment.

Form & Style

Hawthorne pairs a frame essay (“The Custom-House”) with a Puritan-era romance, so the novel literally meditates on its own composition. The narrator is omniscient but ironic, often offering “alternative” interpretations that insist on ambiguity (“The reader may choose…”). Triple scaffold scenes structure the novel: opening (public), midnight (private), and final (confessional). Style is Latinate, periodic, allegorical — every noun threatens to become a capitalized abstraction.

Killer Quotes

  • “On the breast of her gown, in fine red cloth surrounded with an elaborate embroidery and fantastic flourishes of gold thread, appeared the letter A.”
  • “It is to the credit of human nature, that, except where its selfishness is brought into play, it loves more readily than it hates.”
  • “Be true! Be true! Be true!”

Prompt Family Angle

Outward conform / inward question (2005) Dimmesdale = textbook case.
Secrecy (2002-B, 2025-2) The novel’s engine.
Past affecting present (2007) Sin as unhealed inheritance.
Cruelty (2015) Chillingworth’s slow torture; Puritan public shaming.
Justice / injustice (2011) Civic justice vs. moral justice vs. “Pearl’s” justice.

Sample Complex Thesis

Hawthorne’s narrator does not simply contain allegory; he stages its instability, using triple scaffold scenes to dramatize how the same symbol — the scarlet A — passes from civic punishment to private wound to chosen identity, so that Hester’s final return to the cottage and the letter argues that the Puritan order was always less a moral system than a contest over who controls the meanings of signs, and that genuine repentance is not Dimmesdale’s confession but Hester’s authorship.

The Good Lord Bird

James McBride, 2013

Genre — Picaresque historical novel; tragicomedy Setting — Bleeding Kansas → Harpers Ferry, Virginia, 1856–1859 POV — First-person retrospective: Henry “Onion” Shackleford, formerly enslaved boy passing as a girl Form — Frame: a 1966 manuscript discovered in a church fire National Book Award — 2013

Why it dominates AP open prompts: disguise & reinvented identity, the rebel, morally ambiguous protagonist (John Brown himself), unreliable narrator, comic distance from violent material, religious fanaticism as both critique and engine.

Plot in 90 Seconds

In 1856, the abolitionist John Brown raids a Kansas saloon; Onion’s father is killed in the crossfire and Brown — mistaking the boy in his nightshirt for a girl — sweeps “Henrietta” away as his ward. Onion does not correct him; surviving means staying “Henrietta” for almost three years. The novel traces Onion’s travels through the bleeding territories, his time in a Missouri brothel called the Pikesville Hotel, brushes with Frederick Douglass and Harriet Tubman, and culminates in the 1859 raid on Harpers Ferry — a doomed attempt to ignite a slave rebellion. Brown is captured, tried, and hanged; Onion survives, returns to Virginia, and (decades later) writes the manuscript we are reading.

Characters

Character Function
Henry “Onion” Shackleford Picaresque narrator; “passing” boy/girl, Black/free, true/false
John Brown Holy fool; abolitionist saint and dangerous fanatic
Owen Brown John’s son; the practical wing
Frederick Douglass Comically humanized — vain, bookish, incandescent
Harriet Tubman Briefly seen; transcendent, terrifying
Pie Brothel woman who teaches Onion how to “perform” womanhood
Bob Enslaved man whose pragmatism foils Brown’s idealism

Themes & Motifs

  • Reinvention & disguise — gender, race, religion, name.
  • Idealism’s price — Brown is right and destructive.
  • Religious fanaticism — prayer as weapon and weapon as prayer.
  • Comedy as historical method — McBride uses humor to handle violence honestly.
  • Authorship & memory — the frame manuscript, memory’s trickery.
  • The Good Lord Bird itself — feather of grace, ivory-billed woodpecker, sign.

Symbols

The Good Lord Bird (feather) — divine grace; sign of luck given/taken.

Onion’s dress — survival; the lie that becomes the self.

John Brown’s prayers — performance of conviction; the sound of obsession.

Harpers Ferry armory — locked promise; failed ignition.

The onion (vegetable) — layered identity; tears.

The lost manuscript — historiography itself.

Form & Style

McBride writes in a picaresque, vernacular first person that owes a debt to Huckleberry Finn (and openly nods to it). Time is structured by Onion’s retrospective narration from 1942, making the novel an interrogation of historical memory: who gets to tell the story of John Brown? Comedy (slapstick, deflated heroism) is in counterpoint with violence; the frame manuscript is McBride’s quiet move on the American historical novel.

Killer Quotes

  • “The Good Lord Bird don’t run in flocks. He flies alone.”
  • “A man’s life don’t amount to a hill of beans till he’s giving it for something bigger.”
  • “Whatever he believed, he believed. It didn’t matter to him whether it was true or not.”

Open-Prompt Playbook

Prompt Family Angle
Reinvention (2023-1) Onion as Henrietta — gender as survival.
Rebel (2023-2) John Brown literalizes “rebel character.”
Idealism’s cost (2019) Harpers Ferry as catastrophic ideal.
Morally ambiguous (2002) Brown himself, refusing easy verdict.
Stereotype made strange (1975) Douglass and Tubman defamiliarized.
Bildungsroman (2013) Onion’s coming-of-age inside disguise.
Comic / thoughtful laughter (1993) McBride’s tonal control.
Cultural collision (2003-B) Free Black, enslaved, abolitionist white, Native — Bleeding Kansas as collage.

Sample Complex Thesis

McBride’s frame — a Black boy in a dress narrating John Brown — argues that American historical memory is itself a kind of cross-dressing: by routing the 1859 catastrophe through a narrator who is required to perform his identity to stay alive, The Good Lord Bird refuses both the saint-Brown of abolitionist hagiography and the madman-Brown of Lost-Cause revisionism, offering instead a comedy of conviction in which faith is both the weapon and the casualty.

As I Lay Dying

William Faulkner, 1930

Genre — Modernist novel; Southern Gothic; mock-epic Setting — Yoknapatawpha County, Mississippi (fictional), ca. 1928 — the Bundren farm to Jefferson POV — 15 first-person narrators across 59 short chapters Form — Stream of consciousness; multivocal monologue Faulkner wrote it in — six weeks, on overnight shifts at a Mississippi power plant

Why it dominates AP open prompts: physical journey as central structure, modernist time manipulation, multiple unreliable narrators, madness with a “discerning eye,” tragicomedy of the rural poor, a corpse as the inciting object — Faulkner is the answer for almost any prompt about form, distortion, or family.

Plot in 90 Seconds

Addie Bundren, the matriarch of a poor white Mississippi farm family, is dying in her bedroom while her eldest son Cash builds her coffin under the window. Her husband Anse promised to bury her with her people in Jefferson, forty miles away. After her death, the family — Anse, Cash, Darl, Jewel, Dewey Dell, and the youngest, Vardaman — sets out by mule wagon to honor the promise. Floods sweep away a bridge and Cash breaks his leg crossing the river; a barn catches fire (set by Darl) and Jewel rescues the coffin; Dewey Dell, secretly pregnant, tries unsuccessfully to obtain an abortion in two towns. By the time they reach Jefferson, Cash’s leg is rotting under cement, the body has been on the road nine days in summer heat, and Darl has been committed to the asylum at Jackson. Anse buries Addie, borrows shovels from a Mrs. Bundren, and returns with a new wife and a set of false teeth.

Characters

Character Function Key Trait
Addie Bundren The corpse; speaks once, from beyond the grave (Ch. 40) The buried truth: words are “just a shape to fill a lack”
Anse Bundren The patriarch; the ostensible hero of the journey Hypocritical piety; will not sweat; opportunism
Cash Bundren Eldest son; carpenter Methodical; literal; lists 13 reasons for the coffin design
Darl Bundren Second son; the omniscient outlier Visionary, “mad,” the closest narrator to Faulkner himself
Jewel Bundren Third son; Addie’s secret child by Rev. Whitfield Wordless rage; “Jewel’s mother is a horse.”
Dewey Dell Only daughter; pregnant by Lafe Body-as-betrayal; betrayed by druggists
Vardaman Youngest son; child consciousness “My mother is a fish.”
Rev. Whitfield Addie’s lover, Jewel’s father Religious hypocrisy unrepented
Cora Tull / Vernon Tull Neighbors; outside narrators Cora’s certainty vs. Addie’s silence
MacGowan Druggist’s clerk who exploits Dewey Dell Predatory ordinariness

Themes & Motifs

  • Words vs. deeds — Addie’s central insight: “words go straight up in a thin line, quick and harmless, and… terribly doing goes along the earth, clinging to it.”
  • Promise & duty — the entire plot is the keeping of a promise that nobody (except perhaps Jewel) really wanted to make.
  • The body — Addie’s coffin is the family’s center of gravity; their journey is literally burying the past.
  • Existence vs. naming — Vardaman’s “my mother is a fish” is a serious epistemological move, not just a child’s confusion.
  • Class & rural poverty — the Bundrens are the poor whites Faulkner knows; the journey is humiliating because they cannot afford dignity.
  • Hypocrisy — Anse, Whitfield, Cora; everyone borrows piety like Anse borrows tools.
  • Madness as clarity — Darl is committed because he sees too much, not too little.

Symbols

Addie’s coffin — the journey’s literal & symbolic center; Cash builds it “on the bevel.”

The river — biblical crossing; threshold of death and rebirth.

The fire — Darl’s attempt to end the absurd procession; purification.

Anse’s false teeth — the prize for which he endured the journey; capitalism in the casket.

Jewel’s horse — Jewel’s substitute mother and only love; sold by Anse to buy mules.

Vardaman’s fish — child’s metaphor: “My mother is a fish.”

Cash’s tools / level — the carpenter’s certainty against the family’s chaos.

Dewey Dell’s $10 — money for an abortion; stolen by Anse to buy his teeth.

The new Mrs. Bundren — the duck-shaped woman who replaces Addie within hours.

Form & Style

The novel’s form is the argument. Fifteen narrators speak in 59 chapters; some chapters are a single sentence (“My mother is a fish.”), others are baroque interior monologues. Time is splintered — events overlap, are revisited, contradicted. Faulkner uses italics to mark interior thought, and the white space of an unfilled coffin diagram appears on the page in Cash’s chapter. Addie speaks only once, from beyond death (Chapter 40), which retroactively reorganizes everything we have been told. The novel’s title comes from Odyssey Book XI: Agamemnon’s shade laments that Clytemnestra would not close his eyes “as I lay dying.” This is mock-epic — the Odyssey in a mule wagon.

Killer Quotes

  • “My mother is a fish.” (Vardaman, Ch. 19 — the entire chapter)
  • “Jewel’s mother is a horse.”
  • “Words are no good; words don’t ever fit even what they are trying to say at.” (Addie)
  • “He had a word, too. Love, he called it.” (Addie on Anse)
  • “How do our lives ravel out into the no-wind, no-sound, the weary gestures wearily recapitulant…” (Darl)
  • “Yes she did. She told me. God’s will be done. Now I can get them teeth.” (Anse)

Sample Complex Thesis

Faulkner’s fifteen-narrator architecture is itself an ethical argument: by allowing Anse to be heard alongside Darl, Cora alongside Addie, and the corpse alongside the living, As I Lay Dying refuses the consolation of a single moral perspective and instead insists that the rural poor are denied even the dignity of one telling — so that Darl’s commitment at Jackson is not the punishment of madness but the silencing of the only voice that recognized the journey for what it was, while Anse’s new teeth, gleaming on the last page, are the novel’s quiet, devastating verdict on which form of speech the world finally rewards.
